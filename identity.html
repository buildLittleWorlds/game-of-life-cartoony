A tiny ship, a moving pattern, and an ancient question about identity through change.

The big idea

A shape can outlast its pieces

Life makes the Ship of Theseus puzzle cleaner and stranger: a recognizable organization persists even when its visible components change—but unlike a wooden ship, a Life pattern has no physical parts moving through the world.

Is identity matter? Is it form? Is it an unbroken history?

An old wooden puzzle

The Ship of Theseus

Imagine a famous wooden ship kept in service for years. Whenever a plank rots, it is replaced. Eventually none of the original wood remains, but the repaired vessel has kept its name, shape, purpose, and continuous history. Is it still the same ship?

A later twist makes the puzzle harder: suppose someone saves every discarded plank and reassembles them into another ship. The repaired vessel has continuity; the reconstructed vessel has the original material. Which one, if either, is Theseus’s ship?

A ship with no planks

A glider made of moments

A glider takes four generations to return to its starting orientation, one square diagonally away. We call that one glider moving—but no living square makes the journey. Each grid location simply becomes alive or dead in response to its neighbors.

A useful comparison—up to a point

Where the analogy works, and where it breaks

✓ Where it works

  • Organization outlasts constituents. We track a whole even as its visible components change.
  • History matters. Continuous, step-by-step causation supports the intuition that one thing persists.
  • Identity can belong to a process. The enduring object may be a pattern unfolding through time.
  • The observer supplies a boundary. We decide which changing cells count as one coherent entity.

× Where it breaks

  • Cells are places, not planks. A grid location changes state; no piece of matter is carried forward.
  • The rules never declare identity. B3/S23 determines the future, not which configurations are “the same.”
  • Perfect copies can coexist. This separates a pattern type from each individual instance or history.
  • Patterns merge and split. Collisions, debris, and offspring make ancestry and boundaries ambiguous.
  • A real ship has a social story. Name, ownership, purpose, and memory matter in ways a glider lacks.
